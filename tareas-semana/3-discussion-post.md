# Initial Post

Chapter 7 of College Success argues that college develops several kinds of higher-level thinking, including creative, analytical, critical, and problem-solving thinking, along with metacognition and information literacy (Baldwin, 2020). Honestly, no assignment has felt completely pointless so far, but the one I questioned most was the Week 5 academic planning assignment, which asked me to plan my courses and semesters toward my degree. At the time, it felt like busywork. I already knew what I wanted to study, and planning years ahead seemed early.

Looking back, the assignment practiced metacognition, which Baldwin (2020) describes through three processes: planning, tracking, and assessing one's own understanding. Building a schedule required planning, and reviewing that schedule against my degree goals is tracking. Sequencing courses around prerequisites also applied the problem-solving steps of thinking of multiple possible results and selecting the best one (Baldwin, 2020). The Vanderbilt University Center for Teaching (n.d.) lists plan among the creating-level skills in the revised Bloom's taxonomy, so the assignment asked for more than recall.

These skills carry over to finance. Academic planning works like financial planning: I set a goal, forecast what it requires, track progress against the plan, and adjust when something changes. I already practice this in part, on a smaller scale. I set the rates and manage payments for the party entertainment business I run with my dad, including paying our partners and him. Planning ahead is the same skill, whether the goal is a degree or a business.
